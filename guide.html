How to read this page

Written for someone who does not follow football. If you read one thing on a game, read "In plain words."

What the app does

Every day it reads what the betting market thinks of every game, writes it down in plain words, freezes that at kickoff, and after the game scores everyone who made a call: the market, the app's own numbers, the CBS writers, and each person who picked here. The point is not to predict games. The point is that after a few weeks you know who is worth listening to.

The forecast for the stadium at kickoff, from Open-Meteo. A game is flagged, and the Weather filter keeps it, when any one of these is true. Indoor stadiums are never flagged.

Continuity, the injury score

Eleven players are on the field, but they work in small groups that have to read the same picture and call the same thing at the same moment. Continuity is the app's injury score with one twist: it cares whether the injured players belong to the same group. Two missing from one group cost more than two missing from different groups, because the replacements have to learn to talk to each other, not only do their own jobs. Those are the "linked flags" on the card.

GroupWho is in itWhat they coordinate
Protection unitquarterback, the five blockers in front of him, tight end, running backwho blocks whom on every snap, so the quarterback is not hit
Coverage unitcornerbacks, safeties, linebackers: the defenders who guard the players trying to catch passeswho covers which receiver, and the hand-offs when receivers cross paths. One missed hand-off is a wide-open catch
Defensive frontthe big men on the defensive line, plus linebackers: the defenders who meet the run at the linewho fills which gap when the ball is handed off. One empty gap is a long run

Every player on the injury list gets a weight for how likely he is to miss the game (out counts fully, doubtful three quarters, questionable about a third) times a weight for how much his position matters, times a weight for his role. A man listed first at his slot on ESPN's depth chart counts fully. A backup counts about a third. A name not on the chart at all counts a quarter. A player on injured reserve for more than a week counts almost nothing, because his replacement has been practicing with the group all along. Groups with two or more flagged starters add a penalty that grows with the group; backups and long-settled absences never form a group on their own. Playing away or in rain adds a little. The score runs from 100, nobody missing, down to 35: Stable above 82, Watch from 65 to 82, High below 65. The gap between the two teams nudges the win chance by at most 2.5 percentage points and can never flip the favorite. Tap a group's flags on the card to see the names behind it, and "counted lightly" to see who was set aside and why. The weights were chosen by hand, not fitted to results, which is why the cap is low and the card says Experimental; the scorecard decides whether it stays.

Preparation disruption, the practice-week score

Continuity asks who is missing on game day. Preparation asks who did not get ready this week. Teams practice Wednesday, Thursday, and Friday and must publish, after each session, who practiced fully, limited, or not at all. The app reads the wording ESPN attaches to each name and sorts it into categories.

What the report saysWeight
Did not practice1.0
Suspended, on leave, or inactive for the game1.0
Away for personal reasons, not an injury0.55
Limited practice0.5
Activated late in the week, little time with the team0.45

Each weight is multiplied by how much the position matters and by a little more for each extra missed day, up to three. A flag that stops appearing fades by about a third a day and is gone after a week. The same group penalty as Continuity applies. The nudge is smaller, at most 1.5 percentage points, and it never flips the favorite. It depends on ESPN's wording: a note phrased differently is not seen. The same player can count in both scores; the two caps together stay under four points.

The rating model, the second opinion

The app's own attempt to rate teams from what happens on the field, without looking at the betting line. It is shown on every card and graded every week, and it is never used in the forecast, because it lost the test below.

It is built from every play of the 2022 to 2025 seasons. Each play carries a number called expected points added: how much that one play moved the team's chances of scoring. A 40-yard pass is a big positive; a sack on third down is a big negative. Three numbers per team come out of it, computed fresh before each game.

PiecePlain meaning
Offensepoints added per play, corrected for how good the defenses they faced were
Defensepoints allowed per play, corrected the same way, sign flipped so higher is better
Quarterbackhow the named starter's own passing compares with the passing his team's rating was built on. Zero when the same man keeps starting; the point is a change at quarterback, which is why the depth chart feeds it

Recent games count more than old ones: a game 16 back counts half as much as the latest, and last season about a third as much as this one. A team with few plays is pulled toward league average so a hot September does not run away. A simple fit on 2022 to 2024 learns how many points those three differences are worth; that is "Play-level rating margin" on the card. A second fit learns how to mix that with the market line; that is "Blend with the line."

Why the model can rate a champion as average

The model is built from regular-season plays only. A playoff run, the Super Bowl included, is four games it never sees. It also measures points added per play, adjusted for the opponents faced and weighted toward recent games, which is a real signal but not the same thing as winning: a team can be ordinary play by play and still win when it matters. So the model can rank a champion in the bottom third while the market, which prices everything including who won, has the same team as a favorite. Adding the playoff plays was tested on the 2025 replay and made the model slightly worse, 59.4 percent of winners right instead of 60.9, so they stay out.

The card shows home-field advantage inside its number. The model values home field at about 2 points, so "SEA by 0.6" can mean the model would take the visitor on a neutral field. When the model's ranking and the market disagree this much, trust the market; the blind test on 2025 is the reason. The model stays on the page to be graded next to the market every week, and if it keeps losing that comparison it comes off.

Winner and against the spread

Two different calls on the same game, and the app grades both. Winner is the plain question: who wins, by any score. Against the spread is the question with the head start built in. Take Atlanta at Pittsburgh with the line at Pittsburgh by 3.5.

You pick, against the spreadYou are right if
PittsburghPittsburgh wins by 4 or more.
AtlantaAtlanta wins, or loses by 3 or less.

So picking the underdog against the spread is not saying they will win. It is saying they will keep it closer than the line. Picking the favorite is saying they will win by more than the market expects. The line is set so that either side is close to a coin flip, which makes this the harder column and the one that means something: anyone who beats the spread more than about 53 times in 100 over a season has a real edge, and almost nobody does. The CBS writers are graded on it too. You do not have to pick it; winner only is a fine way to play the first weeks.
